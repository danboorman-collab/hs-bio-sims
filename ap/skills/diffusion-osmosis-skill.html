From six masses to a water potential

Percent change in mass, a best-fit line, its zero crossing, and Ψs = −iCRT.

A core gains mass when water moves into it and loses mass when water moves out. Somewhere between the six solutions there is one whose water potential matches the potato's, and in that one the core would neither gain nor lose.

That concentration is not one of the six. It is read off the line through them.

Step 1 — percent change for the first core

A gain of 1 g means more for a small core than for a large one, so the change is compared with the mass the core started at. Keep the sign: it says which way the water went.

Why this step: The cores were not all the same size, so their gains in grams cannot be compared directly. Turning each change into a percentage of what that core started at puts all six on the same footing, which is what lets one line be drawn through them.

percent change = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100

Step 3 — read the zero crossing

Where the line crosses zero, a core would neither gain nor lose mass. So at that concentration the solution's water potential and the potato's are the same.

Why this step: None of the six solutions matched the potato: five made it gain or lose and the sixth would have too. The concentration that matches is read off the line between them, which is how all six cores contribute to an answer that none of them measured.

Step 4 — the temperature in kelvin

The equation needs a temperature in kelvin, which counts up from the coldest anything can be. The steps are the size of a Celsius degree, so add 273.

Why this step: The equation needs a temperature in kelvin, and giving it one in Celsius is the commonest way this calculation goes wrong — it produces an answer about fourteen times too small. Kelvin counts up from the coldest anything can be, and its steps are the same size as Celsius degrees.

Step 5 — the solute potential

i is the number of particles one dissolved unit splits into, and sucrose does not split, so i = 1. R is the pressure constant from the formula sheet, 0.0831 L·bar/(mol·K). The minus sign at the front makes every answer negative, as a solute potential must be.

Why this step: This is the answer the whole investigation was for. At the zero crossing the solution and the potato have the same water potential, so working out the solution's gives the potato's — a property of the tissue that no balance could weigh directly.
