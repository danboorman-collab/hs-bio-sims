How far off might the mean be?

Mean, standard deviation, standard error, and whether two means really differ.

Five tubes at one temperature give five rates that are close but not equal. Their mean is an estimate of the true mean — the rate the mean would reach if the rate were measured in endless tubes.

Step 1 — the mean

The mean is the one rate that stands for all five tubes: add the readings up and divide the total by how many there are.

Why this step: Five tubes at one temperature give five different rates, and none of them is the answer on its own. The mean is the one rate that stands for all five, and it is what every later step is built on — the spread, the standard error, the error bar and the comparison with another temperature all start from it.

x̄ = Σxi ÷ n

Step 2 — the standard deviation

The standard deviation, s, says how far a single reading typically sits from the mean. Take each reading away from the mean, square what is left, add the squares, divide by one less than the number of readings, and take the square root.

Why this step: The mean on its own hides how scattered the five tubes were. Two sets of tubes can share a mean and have nothing else in common. The standard deviation puts one number on that scatter, and the standard error in the next step is worked out from it.

s = √( Σ(xi − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1) )

Step 3 — the standard error of the mean

The five readings are scattered, so the five-tube mean can land a little above or below the true mean. The standard error measures how far off it is likely to be: a wider spread makes it larger, and more tubes make it smaller.

Why this step: The five tubes are a sample, not the whole story. Set up five more and the mean would come out a little different. The standard error says how far a five-tube mean is likely to land from the true mean — the rate you would get from endless tubes — and that is what the error bar is drawn from.

SE = s ÷ √n

Step 4 — two standard errors

An AP graph draws its error bars two standard errors above the mean and two below. That range is where the true mean is likely to lie, and it is called a 95% confidence interval.

Why this step: An AP graph draws its error bars two standard errors each way, not one. That range holds the true mean about 95 times in 100, which is what makes it worth comparing with another bar. One standard error either way would hold it only about two thirds of the time.

Step 5 — the bottom of the error bar

The error bar runs from the mean minus 2SE to the mean plus 2SE.

Why this step: The error bar is the range the true mean is likely to lie in, so it needs two ends. This is the bottom one. Where two bars are compared, it is the bottom of the higher bar that decides whether they overlap.

bar ends = x̄ ± 2SE

What is the verdict on the null hypothesis for 20 °C against 30 °C?

Both verdicts, from one set of tubes. Between 20 and 30 °C the bars are clear of each other, so the null hypothesis is rejected and temperature changed the rate. Between 30 and 40 °C they overlap, so these five tubes cannot say which temperature is the faster — which is also the honest answer about where catalase's optimum lies.
