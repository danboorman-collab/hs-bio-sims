Does the census fit?

p and q from a census, the counts Hardy–Weinberg predicts, and the verdict.

The Hardy–Weinberg model says what a population's genotype counts would look like if nothing were changing its allele frequencies: no selection, no mutation, no migration, random mating, and a large population.

Five conditions that are never all true. That is what makes the model useful: where a census does not fit it, something is going on.

Step 1 — count the B alleles

Hardy–Weinberg is about alleles, not about snails. Each BB snail carries two B alleles, each Bb snail carries one, and each bb snail carries none.

Step 3 — p

p is the share of the alleles that are B.

p = B alleles ÷ all alleles

What is p, to two decimal places?

p500 ÷ 1000

Step 4 — q

There are only two alleles, so every allele that is not B is b.

p + q = 1

What is q?

q1 − 0.50

Step 5 — the expected number of BB snails

A snail is BB when it draws a B from each parent. If mating is at random, the chance of that is p × p.

p² + 2pq + q² = 1

How many BB snails does Hardy–Weinberg predict out of 500?

Step 6 — the expected number of Bb snails

A snail is Bb two ways round: B from the mother and b from the father, or the other way about. That is why the middle term carries a 2.

How many Bb snails does Hardy–Weinberg predict out of 500?

Step 7 — chi-square

One line per genotype class: observed minus expected, squared, divided by that class's own expected count.

χ² = Σ (o − e)² ÷ e

What is chi-square for this census?

Step 8 — degrees of freedom

Degrees of freedom are the number of classes minus one, the same rule as for a breeding cross. The classes here are the three genotypes.

Step 11 — the suspect

There are -50 fewer faintly banded snails than the model predicts, and more of both homozygotes. A follow-up study at the same site found that a snail is far more likely to mate with a snail whose shell looks like its own.

Which Hardy–Weinberg condition does that observation break?

χ² = 20.0, above the critical value of 5.99 at 2 degrees of freedom. Reject the null hypothesis. The census does not fit Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium: there are -50 fewer heterozygotes than the model predicts, and more of both homozygotes. The test says the counts do not fit.
