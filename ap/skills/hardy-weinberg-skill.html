Does the census fit?

p and q from a census, the counts Hardy–Weinberg predicts, and the verdict.

The Hardy–Weinberg model says what a population's genotype counts would look like if nothing were changing its allele frequencies: no selection, no mutation, no migration, random mating, and a large population.

Five conditions that are never all true. That is what makes the model useful: where a census does not fit it, something is going on.

Step 1 — count the B alleles

Hardy–Weinberg is about alleles, not about snails. Each BB snail carries two B alleles, each Bb snail carries one, and each bb snail carries none.

Why this step: Hardy–Weinberg is a statement about alleles, not about snails, so the census has to be converted from one to the other. Every snail carries two alleles; which two depends on its genotype.

Step 3 — p

p is the share of the alleles that are B.

Why this step: p is the whole model's starting point. Everything the model predicts — p², 2pq and q² — is built from it, and it is worked out from this census rather than assumed.

p = B alleles ÷ all alleles

Step 5 — the expected number of BB snails

A snail is BB when it draws a B from each parent. If mating is at random, the chance of that is p × p.

Why this step: This is the first of the three numbers the model predicts. A snail is BB when it draws a B from each parent, and if mating is at random the chance of two independent draws both being B is p times p.

p² + 2pq + q² = 1

Step 6 — the expected number of Bb snails

A snail is Bb two ways round: B from the mother and b from the father, or the other way about. That is why the middle term carries a 2.

Why this step: The heterozygote term carries a 2 because there are two ways to be Bb: B from the mother and b from the father, or the other way about. Forgetting that 2 is the commonest slip in this calculation, and it is also where this census departs most from the model.

The census beside the expectation

Step 7 — chi-square

One line per genotype class: observed minus expected, squared, divided by that class's own expected count.

Why this step: The three expected counts are now beside the three observed ones, and the eye can see they differ. Chi-square turns that impression into one number, so the difference can be judged against what chance alone would produce rather than by how large it looks.

χ² = Σ (o − e)² ÷ e

Step 8 — degrees of freedom

Degrees of freedom are the number of classes minus one, the same rule as for a breeding cross. The classes here are the three genotypes.

Why this step: The same rule as for a breeding cross, and it is worth saying plainly because it looks as though it should be different here: the classes are the three genotypes, so the degrees of freedom are three minus one. The 500 snails play no part.

Step 11 — the suspect

There are -50 fewer faintly banded snails than the model predicts, and more of both homozygotes. A follow-up study at the same site found that a snail is far more likely to mate with a snail whose shell looks like its own.

Which Hardy–Weinberg condition does that observation break?

χ² = 20.0, above the critical value of 5.99 at 2 degrees of freedom. Reject the null hypothesis. The census does not fit Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium: there are -50 fewer heterozygotes than the model predicts, and more of both homozygotes. The test says the counts do not fit. What else is known about the snails is what points at non-random mating.
