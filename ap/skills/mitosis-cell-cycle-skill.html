From counts to times

A share of the cells, a share of the cycle, and the minutes each phase takes.

Every cell on the slide was fixed at the same moment, wherever it happened to be in its cycle. A phase that lasts longer catches more cells in it.

So the share of the cells found in a phase is the share of the cycle that phase takes — which turns a count into a time, once the length of the cycle is known from elsewhere.

Step 1 — a count as a percent

A percent is a count out of a hundred, so a count out of any other total has to be turned into one before two slides can be compared.

percent = count ÷ total × 100

What percent of the cells were in interphase?

Percent in interphase142 ÷ 200 × 100%

What percent of the cells were in anaphase?

Step 3 — the cycle in minutes

The share has to be taken of something to become a time. For these root-tip cells the whole cycle is known to take 24 hours.

How many minutes is 24 hours?

The whole cycle24 × 60min

Step 4 — the time in interphase

time in a phase = percent ÷ 100 × cycle length

How many minutes does interphase take, to one decimal place?

Step 8 — what the method assumes

The share of cells in a phase gives the share of the cycle only if one thing is true of the tissue. Which?

Interphase 1022 minutes, and the whole of mitosis 418. 200 cells, frozen at one moment, give the length of every phase — but only because the cycle length was known from elsewhere, and only because the cells in a root tip divide steadily rather than in step.
