A student cuts five potato cylinders with one cork borer, weighs each at 5.00 g, and leaves one cylinder in each of five sucrose concentrations for half an hour. Which is the independent variable?

  1. The concentration of sucrose each cylinder sits in.
  2. The mass each cylinder has lost or gained at the end.— that is the dependent variable, and it is found by weighing twice
  3. The starting mass of each of the five cylinders.— every cylinder started at 5.00 g, so that is a control variable
  4. The cork borer the five cylinders were cut with.— one borer was used for all five, so that is a control variable

Why: One cylinder went into each of five concentrations. The concentration is what differs from cylinder to cylinder.

Form BBIOP-110 · item 3 of 10 · variant C

A student keeps the lamp 20 cm from the tube of pondweed at every brightness setting. Why does that distance have to be held the same?

  1. Moving the lamp changes the light reaching the pondweed, so two things would change at once.
  2. The lamp warms the water in the tube a good deal more when it is standing closer to it.— the beaker of water between them takes the heat out, which is what it is there for
  3. The bubbles are harder to see and count when the lamp is further away from the tube.— the bubbles are counted in the tube and are no harder to see at 20 cm than at 40 cm
  4. Pondweed stops photosynthesizing altogether if the lamp is moved at all during a run of counting.— it responds to the light it gets; it does not stop because something moved

Why: Brightness is the variable being changed. If the distance moved too, a rise in the bubble count could be put down to either.

Form CBIOP-120 · item 3 of 10 · variant D

A student counts the bubbles from a tube of hydrogen peroxide and catalase for 60 seconds at each of five temperatures. Why is the counting time the same every time?

  1. A count over a longer time is larger for that reason alone, so the temperatures could not be compared.
  2. Catalase is used up by the reaction it speeds up, and 60 seconds is as long as one tube of it lasts for.— an enzyme is not used up by the reaction it speeds up
  3. The reaction only produces bubbles during the first 60 seconds after the mixing.— bubbles keep coming while there is hydrogen peroxide left to break down
  4. Counting for longer than 60 seconds would warm the mixture above the set temperature.— the water bath holds the tube at the set temperature however long the count runs

Why: The count has to stand for a rate, and a rate needs the same length of time behind every reading.

Form DBIOP-130 · item 3 of 10 · variant E

Every potato cylinder in a sucrose investigation is blotted the same way before it is weighed. Why does the blotting have to be done the same way each time?

  1. Solution left on a cylinder is weighed with it, so the mass changes without any osmosis.
  2. Blotting removes the sucrose that has entered the potato cells during the half hour in the tube.— blotting takes liquid off the outside; it reaches nothing inside the cells
  3. A cylinder that is not blotted carries on gaining mass while it is being weighed.— it is out of the solution, so nothing is left to move into it
  4. The balance cannot give a reading at all while a cylinder is still wet from the tube.— a balance weighs a wet cylinder perfectly well; the reading is just not the one wanted

Why: The measurement is meant to be the water that crossed the membrane. Liquid on the outside is counted as if it had.

Form EBIOP-140 · item 3 of 10 · variant B

In the potato and sucrose investigation every cylinder is cut with the same cork borer. What would go wrong if cylinders of different widths were used instead?

  2. The cylinders would no longer fit inside the test tubes that hold the sucrose solutions.— a cylinder of any reasonable width fits; the fault is in the comparison, not the fit

Form ABIOP-100 · item 6 of 10 · variant D

In the onion cell investigation the goggles go on before the dropper of iodine is picked up, rather than once the slide is ready. Why does the order matter?

  1. The stain can reach the eyes the moment it is out of its bottle.
  2. Iodine evaporates and must be used within seconds of opening.— the risk is the liquid reaching skin or eyes, not the time it keeps
  3. Goggles are hard to put on once there is iodine on the hands.— that is a reason to be careful, but it is not what the hazard is
  4. The slide has to be mounted with the goggles already on.— mounting is not the hazardous step; letting the stain out of the bottle is

Why: A precaution has to be in place before the hazard is, not after. Once the dropper is out, a splash is possible.

Form EBIOP-140 · item 6 of 10 · variant C

A student looking down the eyepiece of a microscope turns the focus so that the objective lens moves away from the slide rather than toward it. Why that way?

  1. Racking toward the slide can drive the lens into it, breaking the coverslip and scratching the lens.
  2. Moving away from the slide is the only direction that brings a specimen into focus at all.— focus can be reached from either side; the rule is about what happens if it is overshot
  3. Moving toward the slide makes the image dimmer, because the lens blocks the lamp below it.— the brightness barely changes, and the rule exists because of contact rather than light
  4. Moving away from the slide raises the magnification, which is what a student wants first.— magnification is set by the lens chosen, not by the focusing knob

Form ABIOP-100 · item 7 of 10 · variant A

A student takes three readings at every temperature rather than one. What does having three readings make possible?

  1. A reading that disagrees with the other two can be spotted and left out.
  2. The reaction can be made to run at the same rate in all three runs.— repeating a reading does not change how the reaction runs
  3. The temperature of the water bath can be held steadier than with one run.— the bath is held by the hotplate, and the number of runs does not affect it
  4. The result stops depending on the volume of hydrogen peroxide used.— the volume is held the same anyway; repeats do not remove a variable

Why: One reading cannot be checked against anything. Three can be compared with each other, and a reading that stands apart from the others shows up.

Form BBIOP-110 · item 7 of 10 · variant E

A student lays ten quadrats in a field rather than one, and works out the mean number of plants per quadrat. Why are ten used?

  1. One quadrat might land on a crowded or an empty patch, and ten even that out.
  2. Ten quadrats cover the whole of the field between them.— ten 0.5 m quadrats cover 2.5 m², which is a small part of any field
  3. A mean can only be worked out from ten or more counts.— a mean can be taken from any number of counts
  4. Ten quadrats make the plants spread themselves out more evenly.— laying a frame on the ground does not move the plants

Why: A field is patchy, and one frame samples one patch. Ten frames at random coordinates give a mean that stands for the field rather than for one spot.

Form CBIOP-120 · item 7 of 10 · variant C

A student puts three potato cylinders in each sucrose concentration instead of one. What does that gain?

  1. A cylinder that behaves unlike the other two in its solution can be identified.
  2. Three cylinders take up three times as much water as one would.— that is true of the total and tells you nothing about the percentage change
  3. The concentration of the solution stays steadier with three cylinders in it.— three cylinders draw more from the solution, if anything, rather than less
  4. The cylinders no longer need to be cut to the same size as each other.— they still do; repeats do not remove the need for a control variable

Why: With one cylinder per concentration there is nothing to compare it with. With three, a cylinder that was cut badly or blotted badly shows up.
